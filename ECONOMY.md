# The Economy of Contribution and Provision

The founding economic idea: **I do work that benefits my community, and when I need grain, I receive grain.** Conventional wages and the fear of destitution should not be the only ways to organize productive life.

This draws on some communist and mutual-aid ideas while rejecting compulsory labor under a permanent ruling class.

## Work should be meaningful

People should have a real opportunity to find what they love, learn a craft, and excel at it. Social rank, inherited privilege, and other arbitrary barriers should not close the door. **Opportunity is real training, transparent qualifications, and access to a fair chance, not a guarantee of success or the creation of unlimited positions.**

If a child dreams of being a physician, farmer, carpenter, musician, scientist, or astronaut, the community should not dismiss their ambition simply because of who their parents are. Their ability and willingness to do the work matter.

**But an aspiration is not an unlimited claim on common resources.** The founder expressly gave priority to the material needs of people living on Earth and doubted the social value of costly astronaut careers or prestige spaceflight. Adara therefore asks of every resource-intensive occupation: What benefit does it offer this community and those who come after us? This is a priority question, not a claim that all space-related technology is useless.

## A common storehouse

A community records what it grows and produces, keeps reserves against poor harvests, and distributes essentials according to need. Farmers, millers, bakers, caregivers, teachers, and builders depend on one another without every transaction needing individual wages.

The aim is to prevent both the waste of talent and the fear that losing a job necessarily means losing access to food or shelter.

## Principles of implementation

- Offer accessible education, training, apprenticeships, and opportunities to change vocation.
- Coordinate essential work transparently; invite people into jobs that fit their skills and interests.
- Treat caregiving, teaching, and other often-unpaid work as contributions.
- Make access to essentials dependable, including for those unable to work.
- Track supply, demand, shortages, and reserves publicly.
- Use safer tools, shorter rotations, recognition, and incentives to fill unpopular tasks; do not presume a right to force people into jobs.
- Preserve room for voluntary recreation, creativity, discovery, and nonessential interests without automatically devoting scarce community resources to them.

## Questions that must be solved

**What about jobs few people want?** Sanitation, dangerous repairs, and unpleasant seasonal labor still need volunteers or fair rotation arrangements; a compulsory assignment would clash with personal sovereignty.

**What about shortages?** Equal access is not the same as unlimited supply. Open information and transparent rationing may be necessary.

**What about free riders?** Distinguishing inability from refusal is difficult. Food and human dignity should not become bargaining chips for compliance.

**What about choice?** Communities can offer opportunities without promising that every dream receives a publicly funded workplace. Allocation decisions must be reviewable and constrained by justice and reciprocity.

**What about outsiders?** A single community cannot assume it can produce everything. Exchanges and material accounting still matter.
