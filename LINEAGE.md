# Philosophical Lineage: A Deliberate Mixture

Adara is a new combination of longstanding ideas, not a claim to have invented those ideas. These sources are explanatory references, **not** authorities that automatically determine Adara's tenets. The distinctions are as important as the similarities.

Adara makes no theological claims and does not ask believers to abandon their faith. It is a philosophy of conduct toward other people, compatible with religious or nonreligious convictions; Earth is the setting of human responsibility, not a being to worship.

## 1. Altruism — concern for others

**Idea:** People can be motivated by another person's welfare for its own sake.

**How Adara uses it:** Common Benefit and Duty of Contribution ask whether an activity helps or harms others, regardless of the status or wealth it brings its organizer.

**Where Adara differs:** It does not demand the abolition of personal joy, hobbies, or private ambition.

**Source:** [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Altruism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/altruism/).

## 2. Consequentialism — pay attention to results

**Idea:** The consequences of an action matter to its moral assessment.

**How Adara uses it:** The Everest example and the opium-trade comparison challenge the idea that providing jobs makes all industries socially valuable.

**Where Adara differs:** Sovereignty and Reciprocity limit what may be done to an individual in the name of the majority's supposed benefit.

**Source:** [Stanford Encyclopedia: Consequentialism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consequentialism/).

## 3. Communitarianism — shared life and responsibility

**Idea:** Communities shape and sustain their members; obligations are not simply a matter of individual contracts.

**How Adara uses it:** Mutual contribution, communal justice, the value of elder knowledge, and recognition of caregiving.

**Where Adara differs:** A community does not gain unlimited authority over its members merely because it is a community.

**Source:** [Stanford Encyclopedia: Communitarianism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/communitarianism/).

## 4. Philosophical anarchism — question claims of authority

**Idea:** Political authority needs justification; cooperation does not necessarily require permanent rulers.

**How Adara uses it:** Its founding declaration that nobody has an inherent right to rule another, and its insistence on voluntary cooperation.

**Where Adara faces a challenge:** Binding communal tribunals have coercive power. They need clear protections, constraints, and accountability to avoid becoming rulers under another name.

**Source:** [Stanford Encyclopedia: Anarchism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/anarchism/).

## 5. Restorative justice and Diné peacemaking — repair relationships

**Idea:** Justice can include repairing damage, accountability, restoring relationships where appropriate, and transforming conflict.

**How Adara uses it:** Communal Justice emphasizes proportional accountability, restitution, and chances for rehabilitation.

**Important difference:** The Diné (Navajo) Peacemaking Program is a living tradition rooted in its own community, laws, and culture. Adara neither represents Indigenous peoples nor copies a single universal “Native American justice system.”

**Sources:** [Navajo Nation Judicial Branch, public guide to courts and peacemaking](https://courts.navajo-nsn.gov/publicguide.htm) · [Navajo Nation Judicial Branch, Peacemaking Program brochure](https://courts.navajo-nsn.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Peacemaking-Program-Brochure-OCT.2025.pdf).

## 6. Intergenerational justice — duties beyond our lifetime

**Idea:** Decisions today affect future generations, even though they cannot participate in current discussions.

**How Adara uses it:** Betterment, stewardship of land, transmission of skills and useful traditions, and the duty to correct inherited mistakes.

**Source:** [Stanford Encyclopedia: Intergenerational Justice](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/justice-intergenerational/).

## 7. Marx's needs principle and mutual-aid traditions — shared provision

**Idea:** Marx's later-stage communist ideal included the phrase “from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs.” This is not the same thing as a universal ideal of lifelong compulsory job assignment.

**How Adara uses it:** People find useful work, develop their abilities, and share access to necessities instead of receiving a narrow wage for each service.

**Where Adara differs:** The sovereignty tenet rules out treating people as labor owned by a ruling authority. It also prioritizes work that benefits people and communities on Earth over prestige projects with little public use.

**Sources:** [Karl Marx, Critique of the Gotha Programme](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1875/gotha/ch01.htm) · [Stanford Encyclopedia: Needs](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/needs/) · [Stanford Encyclopedia: Socialism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/socialism/) · [Stanford Encyclopedia: Anarchism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/anarchism/).

## The synthesis in one sentence

**Let the needs of others inform your purpose, let shared responsibility guide your contribution, and let individual sovereignty restrain every attempt to turn the common good into domination.**

The tensions remain useful: How does a community enforce restitution without becoming coercive? How do people choose their work when essential jobs go unfilled? How do we balance useful tradition with innovation? The philosophy is built to confront those questions rather than conceal them.
