# Communal Justice and the Role of Elders

The **seventh tenet is adopted**. The founding discussion also explored how such justice might be administered without creating another ruling class.

## The example that forced the question

A hypothetical local strongman threatened to take what he wanted from neighbors during a national collapse and use violence against dissenters. The Sovereignty tenet rejects his premise: strength and circumstance do not give him authority over other people.

But protecting neighbors raises a difficult question. Who judges actual wrongdoing in a community without permanent rulers?

## A communal tribunal

The initial idea was **not a judge plus a six-person jury**, but a community where people share responsibility for verdicts and consequences. It included punishment in proportion to severity, escalating responses to continued offenses, and genuine second chances.

Discussion considered eligibility by maturity and, at one point, voting by males above a certain age. It then questioned whether all community members should vote and whether a council of elders could better provide judgment. **No final voting-age, gender-eligibility, or voting-threshold rule was settled in that conversation.** The seventh tenet itself, however, was expressly accepted.

## Wisdom without unchecked power

Elders were valued for preserving tradition, remembering families' histories, and knowing the costs of past mistakes. At the same time, the founder observed that a small group of elders could act from self-interest or become set in its ways. Younger generations sometimes abandon worthy tradition, but may also recognize what needs correction.

A possible arrangement is an advisory elder council paired with wider participation and independent review. No council should enjoy immunity merely because its members are old or respected.

## Checks against abuse

- Impartiality and recusal when someone has a personal stake in a case.
- Clear charges, relevant evidence, and a real opportunity for the accused to answer.
- Responses proportionate to harm, intent, and repeated misconduct.
- Preference for restitution and correction where these do not expose victims to further harm.
- Appeals and review of mistaken or biased decisions.
- Protection against punishing someone solely because they are unpopular.

The hardest tension is between communal enforcement and the axiom that nobody has an inherent right to rule. A vote is not a license for unjust domination. Procedures must be justified by protecting against genuine harm.

## Context, not appropriation

Traditional forms of Indigenous peacemaking were discussed. The Navajo Nation has its own living Peacemaking Program and judicial institutions. Adara can study its account of restoring relationships without claiming those practices as its own or assuming every Indigenous nation has the same law.
